History of chess>

Chess has a 1,500-year history that spans from ancient Indian battle simulations to a global digital sport. Origins in India (6th Century) • Chaturanga: Chess began in 6th-century India as chaturanga, a Sanskrit name meaning "four divisions" of the military (infantry, cavalry, elephantry, and chariots). • The Pieces: These early units mirrored what would become modern pawns, knights, bishops, and rooks, centered around the survival of a single king. Spread Through Persia and the Islamic World • Shatranj: The game traveled to Persia, where it became known as shatranj or chatrang. Persian and Arabic terms gave rise to modern chess vocabulary, including check and checkmate (from the Persian phrase shāh māt, meaning "the king is helpless"). • Islamic Golden Age: Following the 7th-century conquest of Persia, Islamic scholars analyzed openings, recorded game studies, and wrote some of the earliest strategy treatises. Arrival and Evolution in Medieval Europe • Feudal Pastime: Chess reached Europe via trade routes through Spain, Italy, and Russia around the 9th to 11th centuries, becoming a favorite intellectual pastime for nobility. • The Modern Queen: In the late 15th century (around 1475–1485 in Spain and Italy), the rules shifted dramatically. The counselor piece (ferz) was transformed into the powerful queen, and the elephant became the bishop, vastly increasing the speed and attacking potential of the game. Standardization, Competition, and the World Championship • 19th-Century Innovation: Organized tournaments, chess clocks (which stopped games from dragging on for hours), and standard equipment (such as the Staunton chess set in 1849) formalized competitive play. • World Champions: The first official World Chess Championship took place in 1886, won by Wilhelm Steinitz, who pioneered modern positional theory over purely tactical attacks. The 20th century was marked by long stretches of Soviet dominance, legendary rivalries like Kasparov vs. Karpov, and global icons like Bobby Fischer and Magnus Carlsen. The Digital and Computer Age • Man vs. Machine: A historic turning point occurred in 1997 when IBM's Deep Blue supercomputer defeated reigning world champion Garry Kasparov. • Modern Play: Today, neural-network engines, online chess platforms, and global streaming have driven chess into a massive 21st-century renaissance

How to play chess

Board Setup • Grid: Play on an 8x8 board with 64 alternating light and dark squares. • Orientation: Ensure a light square sits in the bottom-right corner for each player ("light on the right"). • Placement: • Place Rooks in the corners. • Place Knights next to the rooks. • Place Bishops next to the knights. • Place the Queen on the central square matching her color (white queen on white, black queen on black). • Place the King in the remaining square next to the queen. • Line up all 8 Pawns on the row directly in front of the other pieces. • White pieces occupy ranks 1 and 2; black pieces occupy ranks 7 and 8. How the Pieces Move • Pawn: Moves forward 1 square (or 2 squares on its very first move). Captures diagonally forward 1 square. • Rook: Moves any number of squares horizontally or vertically. • Knight: Moves in an "L" shape (two squares in one direction, then one square perpendicular) and is the only piece that can jump over others. • Bishop: Moves any number of squares diagonally. • Queen: Combines the movement powers of the rook and the bishop (any number of squares horizontally, vertically, or diagonally). • King: Moves 1 square in any direction. The Goal and Special Rules • Objective: Checkmate the opponent's king (trapping the king so it cannot escape capture). • White Moves First: Players alternate taking turns moving one piece at a time. • Special Moves: • Castling: A move involving the king and a rook to protect the king. • En Passant: A special diagonal pawn capture against an opponent's pawn that just moved two squares. • Promotion: When a pawn reaches the opposite end of the board, it promotes to a queen, rook, bishop, or knight. • Draws: Games can end in a tie (stalemate, repetition, agreement, or insufficient material).
